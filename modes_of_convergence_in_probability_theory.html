3. Taxonomy of Convergence Modes

Mode Criterion Key Theorem
Almost Sure ($\text{a.s.}$) $P(\lim_{n\to\infty} X_n = X) = 1$ Kolmogorov SLLN, Glivenko-Cantelli
Mean-Square ($L^2$) $\lim_{n\to\infty} \mathbb{E}[(X_n - X)^2] = 0$ $\text{MSE} \to 0$ consistency
In Probability ($\xrightarrow{P}$) $\lim_{n\to\infty} P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) = 0$ Khinchin WLLN, Weak consistency
In Distribution ($\xrightarrow{d}$) $\lim_{n\to\infty} F_{X_n}(x) = F_X(x)$ Lindeberg-Lévy CLT, Slutsky

Implication Hierarchy: $\text{a.s.} \implies \xrightarrow{P} \implies \xrightarrow{d}$ and $L^2 \implies \xrightarrow{P} \implies \xrightarrow{d}$, where $X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} X$ requires $\mathbb{E}[(X_n - X)^2] \to 0$ (not merely $\text{Var}(X_n) \to 0$). Conversely, if $X_n \xrightarrow{d} c$ where $c$ is a deterministic constant, then $X_n \xrightarrow{P} c$; convergence in distribution to a non-degenerate random variable does not imply convergence in probability.

4. Why $\text{a.s.}$ and $L^2$ Do Not Imply Each Other

Typewriter Sequence on $\Omega = [0,1)$: For each integer $m \ge 1$, partition $[0,1)$ into $m$ subintervals $I_{m,j} = \left[\frac{j}{m}, \frac{j+1}{m}\right)$ for $j = 0, \dots, m-1$, and enumerate $X_{m,j}(\omega) = \mathbf{1}_{I_{m,j}}(\omega)$ block by block as a single sequence $X_n$. Then $\mathbb{E}[X_{m,j}^2] = \frac{1}{m} \to 0$ as $m \to \infty$ (so $X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} 0$ and $X_n \xrightarrow{P} 0$). However, every $\omega \in [0,1)$ belongs to one interval in each block $m$, so $X_n(\omega) = 1$ infinitely often and $0$ infinitely often: almost sure convergence fails everywhere.

Mathematical Theory: Modes of Convergence, LLN, CLT & $\sqrt{n}$

Roles in LLN, CLT & Statistical Inference

  • Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN): States $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu$. When $\sigma^2 < \infty$, Chebyshev's inequality yields $P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \to 0$ directly from $L^2$ convergence.
  • Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN): For an i.i.d. sequence with $\mathbb{E}[|X_1|] < \infty$, Kolmogorov's theorem proves that the sample mean converges almost surely to $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X_1]$ ($\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mu$).
  • Central Limit Theorem (CLT): For i.i.d. variables with $0 < \sigma^2 < \infty$, $\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Unlike $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu$, scaling by $\sqrt{n}$ preserves random fluctuations: this describes the asymptotic convergence of the normalized estimator's probability distribution, not pathwise convergence of individual trajectories to a limiting random variable.
  • Slutsky & Glivenko-Cantelli: Distinguishing sample standard deviation $S_n = \sqrt{S_n^2}$ from sample variance $S_n^2$, if $S_n \xrightarrow{P} \sigma > 0$ and $\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, Slutsky's theorem yields $\frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}{S_n} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Glivenko-Cantelli guarantees $\sup_x |F_n(x) - F(x)| \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} 0$.

Why $\sqrt{n}$ Appears: Variance Additivity & Independence

For partial sums $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ with finite second moments, the bilinear expansion of variance is:

$$\text{Var}(S_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n \text{Var}(X_i) + 2\sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} \text{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$$

Pairwise uncorrelatedness ($\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = 0$) is sufficient for variance additivity $\text{Var}(S_n) = n\sigma^2$ and $\text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma^2 / n$. However, uncorrelated variables can still be dependent and violate the classical CLT; thus independence (or structured weak dependence) is required for asymptotic normality. Rescaling by $n^\alpha$ gives:

$$\text{Var}\big(n^\alpha(\bar{X}_n - \mu)\big) = n^{2\alpha - 1}\sigma^2$$

Only at $\alpha = \frac{1}{2}$ ($n^{1/2} = \sqrt{n}$) does $2\alpha - 1 = 0$, keeping the asymptotic variance finite and non-zero. Under serial dependence ($\rho \neq 0$), covariances modify the long-run variance to $\sigma_{\text{LR}}^2 = \sigma^2 \sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty} \rho_X(h)$, changing the asymptotic effective sample size $n_{\text{eff}} = n (\sigma^2 / \sigma_{\text{LR}}^2)$.
