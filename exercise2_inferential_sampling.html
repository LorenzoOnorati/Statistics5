1. Asymptotic Theory & Integrability Conditions (SLLN)

Q1. Integrability Conditions for Almost Sure Convergence

Under Kolmogorov's Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN), moment conditions differ strictly for the mean and the variance:

  • Sample Mean: For i.i.d. observations, $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mu$ if and only if the first moment exists, $\mathbb{E}[|X|] < \infty$.
  • Sample Variance: For i.i.d. observations with finite second moment ($\mathbb{E}[X^2] < \infty$), the sample variance converges almost surely to the population variance: $$S_n^2 \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \sigma^2$$ according to the Strong Law of Large Numbers applied to both $X$ and $X^2$, since $S_n^2 = \frac{n}{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2 - \bar{X}_n^2\right)$.

Q2. Independence vs. Uncorrelated Observations & Equicorrelation

Independence is a sufficient condition, but not a necessary one. Pairwise uncorrelated observations with common finite variance $\sigma^2$ are also sufficient to ensure that the variance of the sample mean decreases as $\sigma^2/n$: $$\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(X_i) + \frac{2}{n^2}\sum_{1 \le i < j \le n}\mathrm{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$ If all distinct pairs have a constant non-zero correlation $\rho \neq 0$, the variance decomposes as: $$\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \frac{n-1}{n}\rho\sigma^2 \implies \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \rho\sigma^2 \neq 0$$ Hence, if distinct pairs share a constant correlation $\rho$, the variance converges to $\rho\sigma^2$ instead of zero.

2. Statistical Conclusions

Q1. Comparison between empirical and theoretical values

Across the $m$ hackers, the average of the sampling means is an estimator of the population mean: $$\bar{\bar{X}} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m \bar{X}_j$$ Its expected value is $\mathbb{E}[\bar{\bar{X}}] = \mu$. Similarly, the average of the $m$ unbiased sample variances estimates the population variance: $$\overline{S^2} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m S_j^2$$ Its expected value is $\mathbb{E}[\overline{S^2}] = \sigma^2$, provided that the observations are i.i.d. and have finite variance. Small differences between empirical and theoretical values are expected because of random sampling fluctuations across finite $m$.

Q2. Effect of increasing the number of systems $n$

As the number of systems attempted by each hacker increases, the variability of the sampling means decreases according to: $$\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$ The variability of the sample variances also decreases, according to its exact theoretical formula: $$\mathrm{Var}(S_n^2) = \frac{1}{n}\left[ \mu_4 - \frac{n-3}{n-1}\sigma^4 \right]$$ Thus, larger sample sizes produce more stable estimates of both the population mean and population variance.

Q3. Importance for inferential statistics

These results illustrate two fundamental principles of inferential statistics: unbiased estimation and consistency.

The sample mean and sample variance provide estimates of unknown population parameters. Increasing the sample size generally reduces estimation uncertainty, making the estimates more reliable.

The Central Limit Theorem also explains why, under suitable assumptions, the sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as the sample size increases. These properties form the theoretical foundation for statistical estimation, confidence intervals, and hypothesis testing.

Theoretical Extension: Confidence Intervals

Under population normality, Student's $t$ interval $\bar{X} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}}$ is exact. For non-normal populations with finite variance, it serves as an asymptotic approximation justified by the Central Limit Theorem and Slutsky's theorem ($S_n \xrightarrow{\mathrm{p}} \sigma$).
