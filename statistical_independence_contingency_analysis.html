Theoretical Foundations: Independence, $\chi^2$ Test & Causality

Independence in Probability & Statistics

Two discrete random variables $X$ and $F$ are statistically independent ($X \perp\!\!\!\perp F$) if and only if their joint probability distribution factorizes into the product of their marginal distributions for all $(i, j)$:

$$P(X = x_i, F = f_j) = P(X = x_i) \cdot P(F = f_j)$$

Equivalently, knowing the value of $X$ provides zero information about $F$, meaning conditional probabilities equal unconditional marginal probabilities:

$$P(F = f_j \mid X = x_i) = \frac{P(X = x_i, F = f_j)}{P(X = x_i)} = P(F = f_j)$$

In a sample of size $N$, replacing theoretical probabilities by empirical marginal frequencies $\hat{p}_{i\bullet} = \frac{n_{i\bullet}}{N}$ and $\hat{p}_{\bullet j} = \frac{n_{\bullet j}}{N}$ yields the expected frequency under $H_0$: $$E_{ij} = N \cdot \hat{p}_{i\bullet} \cdot \hat{p}_{\bullet j} = \frac{n_{i\bullet} \cdot n_{\bullet j}}{N}$$

Pearson's Chi-Square Statistic ($\chi^2$)

To quantify global departure from independence across an $R \times C$ contingency table, Pearson's statistic sums the normalized squared discrepancies between observed counts $O_{ij}$ and expected counts $E_{ij}$:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{R} \sum_{j=1}^{C} \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}$$

Under the null hypothesis $H_0$ of independence and sufficiently large sample sizes, $\chi^2$ converges asymptotically to a chi-square distribution with $\nu = (R - 1)(C - 1)$ degrees of freedom. The $p$-value is given by the upper regularized incomplete gamma function: $$p\text{-value} = P(\chi^2_\nu \ge \chi^2_{\text{obs}}) = Q\left(\frac{\nu}{2}, \frac{\chi^2_{\text{obs}}}{2}\right)$$

Statistical Association vs. Causality

Rejecting the null hypothesis ($p < \alpha$) establishes statistical dependence (association) between $X$ and $F$, meaning their joint behavior deviates systematically from randomness. However, statistical association does not imply causation:

  • Confounding (Lurking) Variables: A third unmeasured variable $Z$ may causally drive both $X$ and $F$ simultaneously. For example, in Preset 1, high Ice Cream Sales ($X$) are strongly associated with Beach Rescue Incidents ($F$), yet neither causes the other: ambient summer temperature ($Z$) increases both.
  • Directionality & Reverse Causation: The $\chi^2$ test is symmetric with respect to $X$ and $F$ ($\chi^2(X, F) = \chi^2(F, X)$) and cannot distinguish whether $X \to F$ or $F \to X$.
  • Statistical Significance vs. Practical Magnitude: For very large $N$, tiny departures from independence yield large $\chi^2$ values. Normalized effect-size metrics such as Cramér's $V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{N \min(R-1, C-1)}} \in [0, 1]$ measure the practical strength of association independently of sample size.
