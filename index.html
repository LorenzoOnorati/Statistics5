Exercise 1 • Recurrence

$\mathcal{O}(1)$ Space • Numerically Stable

For score $x_k$ at system $k=1 \dots n$:

// 1. Difference from old mean
delta = x - mean;
// 2. Knuth mean recurrence
mean += delta / k;
// 3. Welford SS recurrence
M2 += delta * (x - mean);
// Unbiased variance (k ≥ 2)
S2 = (k > 1) ? M2 / (k - 1) : NaN;

*Avoids catastrophic cancellation. For $k=1$, sample variance is mathematically undefined ($0/0$).

Sensitivity Analysis: Increasing Systems $n$ Attempted per Hacker

Exercise 2 investigation: "Repeat the experiment for different values of $n$. How does increasing the number of systems attempted by each hacker affect the variability of the sampling means and sampling variances?"

A. Variability of the Sampling Means $\bar{X}_n$

$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$

As $n$ increases, the variability of the sample mean decreases strictly proportional to $1/n$. Increasing $n$ from 10 to 200 reduces $\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X})$ by exactly a factor of 20.

B. Exact Variability of Sample Variances $S_n^2$

$$\mathrm{Var}(S_n^2) = \frac{1}{n}\left[ \mu_4 - \frac{n-3}{n-1}\sigma^4 \right] \quad (n \ge 2)$$

Variability also decreases as $n$ increases, but it does not decay strictly as $1/n$ for finite $n$. The ratio $\frac{\mathrm{Var}(S_{10}^2)}{\mathrm{Var}(S_{200}^2)} = 20 \cdot \frac{\mu_4 - \frac{7}{9}\sigma^4}{\mu_4 - \frac{197}{199}\sigma^4} \neq 20$.

1. Knuth's Recurrence Formula for the Mean

Let $\bar{x}_k = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^k x_i$ be the sample mean of the first $k$ observations. Decomposing the sum:

$$\bar{x}_k = \frac{1}{k} \left( \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} x_i + x_k \right) = \frac{1}{k} \left( (k-1)\bar{x}_{k-1} + x_k \right)$$ $$\bar{x}_k = \frac{k\bar{x}_{k-1} - \bar{x}_{k-1} + x_k}{k} = \bar{x}_{k-1} + \frac{x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}}{k}$$

Defining $\delta_k = x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}$, we obtain the streaming recurrence: $$\bar{x}_k = \bar{x}_{k-1} + \frac{\delta_k}{k}$$

2. Welford's Recurrence Formula for Sum of Squares ($M_{2,k}$)

Let $M_{2,k} = \sum_{i=1}^k (x_i - \bar{x}_k)^2$ be the sum of squared differences from the updated mean $\bar{x}_k$. Consider the decomposition:

$$M_{2,k} = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (x_i - \bar{x}_k)^2 + (x_k - \bar{x}_k)^2$$

Rewriting $x_i - \bar{x}_k = (x_i - \bar{x}_{k-1}) + (\bar{x}_{k-1} - \bar{x}_k)$ and expanding the summation for $i=1 \dots k-1$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (x_i - \bar{x}_k)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (x_i - \bar{x}_{k-1})^2 + 2(\bar{x}_{k-1} - \bar{x}_k) \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(x_i - \bar{x}_{k-1})}_{= 0 \text{ by definition of } \bar{x}_{k-1}} + (k-1)(\bar{x}_{k-1} - \bar{x}_k)^2$$ $$M_{2,k} = M_{2,k-1} + (k-1)(\bar{x}_{k-1} - \bar{x}_k)^2 + (x_k - \bar{x}_k)^2$$

Using Knuth's mean relation $\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1} = \frac{x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}}{k}$, the remaining two terms simplify identically:

$$(k-1)(\bar{x}_{k-1} - \bar{x}_k)^2 + (x_k - \bar{x}_k)^2 = (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k)$$

Setting $\delta_k = x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}$, we arrive at the numerically stable one-pass recurrence: $$M_{2,k} = M_{2,k-1} + \delta_k (x_k - \bar{x}_k)$$ For any sample of size $k \ge 2$, Bessel's unbiased sample variance is: $$s_k^2 = \frac{M_{2,k}}{k - 1}$$ For $k=1$, $s_1^2$ is mathematically undefined (division by zero); it is handled as non-computable in formal analysis.

3. Inferential Statistics & Asymptotic Theory (Ex. 2)

Q1. Integrability Conditions for Almost Sure Convergence (SLLN)

By Kolmogorov's Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN), we distinguish the moment requirements:

  • Sample Mean: For i.i.d. observations, $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mu$ if and only if the first moment exists, $\mathbb{E}[|X|] < \infty$.
  • Sample Variance: For i.i.d. observations with finite second moment, the sample variance converges almost surely to the population variance: $$S_n^2 \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \sigma^2$$ according to the Strong Law of Large Numbers applied to both $X$ and $X^2$, because $S_n^2 = \frac{n}{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2 - \bar{X}_n^2\right)$ and $\mathbb{E}[X^2] < \infty$.

Q2. Independence Assumption and Pairwise Correlation

Independence is a sufficient condition, but not a necessary one. Pairwise uncorrelated observations with finite identical variance $\sigma^2$ are also sufficient to ensure that the variance of the sample mean decreases as $\sigma^2/n$: $$\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(X_i) + \frac{2}{n^2}\sum_{1 \le i < j \le n}\mathrm{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$ If all distinct pairs have a constant non-zero correlation $\rho \neq 0$ and common variance $\sigma^2$, the variance decomposes as: $$\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \frac{n-1}{n}\rho\sigma^2 \implies \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \rho\sigma^2 \neq 0$$ Hence, the variance converges to $\rho\sigma^2$ instead of zero.

Optional Extension: Inferential Applications (Intervals & Tests)

Under population normality, the Student $t$ confidence interval $\bar{X} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}}$ is exact. For non-normal populations with finite variance, it serves as an asymptotic approximation justified by Slutsky's theorem and the Central Limit Theorem.

4. Statistical Conclusions

Q1. Comparison between empirical and theoretical values

Across the $m$ hackers, the average of the sampling means is an estimator of the population mean: $$\bar{\bar{X}} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m \bar{X}_j$$ Its expected value is $\mathbb{E}[\bar{\bar{X}}] = \mu$. Similarly, the average of the $m$ unbiased sample variances estimates the population variance: $$\overline{S^2} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m S_j^2$$ Its expected value is $\mathbb{E}[\overline{S^2}] = \sigma^2$, provided that the observations are i.i.d. and have finite variance.

Small differences between empirical and theoretical values are expected because of random sampling fluctuations across finite $m$ trajectories.

Q2. Effect of increasing the number of systems $n$

As the number of systems attempted by each hacker increases, the variability of the sampling means decreases according to: $$\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$ The variability of the sample variances also decreases, according to its exact theoretical formula: $$\mathrm{Var}(S_n^2) = \frac{1}{n}\left[ \mu_4 - \frac{n-3}{n-1}\sigma^4 \right]$$ Thus, larger sample sizes produce more stable estimates of both the population mean and population variance. While the variance of $\bar{X}_n$ decays strictly as $1/n$, the decay of $\mathrm{Var}(S_n^2)$ incorporates higher-order kurtosis corrections for small $n$.

Q3. Importance for inferential statistics

These results illustrate two fundamental principles of inferential statistics: unbiased estimation and consistency.

The sample mean and sample variance provide estimates of unknown population parameters. Increasing the sample size generally reduces estimation uncertainty, making the estimates more reliable.

The Central Limit Theorem also explains why, under suitable assumptions, the sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as the sample size increases. These properties form the theoretical foundation for statistical estimation, confidence intervals, and hypothesis testing.
